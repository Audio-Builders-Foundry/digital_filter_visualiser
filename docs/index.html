Digital Filter Visualizer

an intuitive way to create, visualize and evaluate your digital filter

ABSTRACT


Digital filters are a foundational concept in digital audioIs this working? , but designing them presents certain challenges: from finding the right coefficients to checking for stability.

We present DigitalFilterVisualizer: a tool that allows you to see the mathematical structure of linear time-invariant filters, calculate filter coefficients from your poles and zeros and vice versa, automatically ensure the stability, and instantly hear the result.

DigitalFilterVisualizer features 5 autosynchronized representations of a filter, including interactive visual interfaces, to provide utility in both learning and creating.

A method to convert coefficients to roots is provided using the Francis implicit double shift QR algorithm.

Overview


There are no closed-form solutions for polynomials of degree 5 or greater; iterative methods are required. Algorithms largely fall into one of two broad categories: Newton’s method-based solvers and eigenvalue solvers.

Newton’s method is a technique for finding roots of any function, not just polynomials. While it is relatively simple and can be applied to polynomials as well, Newton’s method has problems with convergence, especially when dealing with complex roots and roots with multiplicity. There are specializations of Newton’s method for polynomials that try to address these problems, such as the Aberth-Ehrlich method, but we found these insufficient.

Eigenvalue solvers use a method for computing the eigenvalues of a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. Compared to Newton solvers, they require more sophisticated mathematical theory to understand, and are seemingly more computationaly expensive; indeed the operations on polynomials in Newton’s method can be implemented in linear time, whereas most operations on matrices require quadratic or cubic time. Yet the eigenvalue formalism offers advantages in analyzing convergence and designing algorithms with improved performance on roots with multiplicity.

We use an Eigenvalue solver using a variant of the QR algorithm called the Francis double implicit shift. Simple QR algorithm implementations have issues with convergence for complex roots and roots with multiplicity, much like Newton solvers, which can be addressed using a technique called shifting. Single shifting is insufficient due to the possibility of complex roots, which necessitates double shifting; explicit double shifting is inefficient, hence the need for implicit double shifts. Orthogonal matrices are constructed as a product of Householder reflections.

The QR solver with double implicit shift finds one or two roots at a time. Our application stores roots along with their orders. When the polynomial has roots with order more than 1, the solver will produce several roots whose weighted average is a close approximation of the true root. Individual roots corresponding to the same high order root may not appear consecutively in the list of solutions, and each root will be farther from the true root position the higher its order. This necessitates a post-processing pass to group individual roots corresponding to the same true root into a single object.

section 2 describes how the QR eigenvalue solver works in general. section 3 details methods for improving the convergence rate of the general QR solver. section 4 contains details about our specific implementation and the post processing technique for clustering higher-order roots.

Strategy


We will construct a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. Then we will iteratively apply operations to this matrix in such a way that we don’t change its eigenvalues, until we have produced a matrix whose eigenvalues we can determine directly from its coefficients.

2.1. Companion Matrix. To start, we need to construct a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. This can be done as follows: given a polynomial p(x) = anxn + + a0, the matrix

Note that the companion matrix has ones along the diagonal below the main diagonal and zeros in all places below this subdiagonal; we call any matrix with this property an upper Hessenberg matrix, or more specifically, an unreduced upper Hessenberg matrix. Finding the eigenvalues of upper Hessenberg matrices is a well-studied problem, and exploiting this structure will play a key role in the theory.

A block upper triangular matrix made up of either 1 × 1 or 2 × 2 matrices is known as a reduced upper Hessenberg matrix. Our goal is to bring the unreduced upper Hessenberg companion matrix to reduced upper Hessenberg form by bringing some subdiagonal entries to zero.

2.3. Similar Matrices. Two square matrices A and B with the same dimension have the same eigenvalues whenever there is a matrix X such that B = X-1AX; we say A and B are similar matrices in this case. Given a matrix A, forming B = X-1AX for some matrix X is called conjugation of A by X. We will refer to this operation as applying a similarity transformation to A. A matrix Q is called orthogonal if QT = Q-1; these are a good choice for use in similarity transformations since it avoids having to form the inverse of a matrix (an expensive operation in general). We will call a similarity transformation using an orthogonal matrix an orthogonal similarity transformation.

2.4. QR Decomposition. So we want to find the roots of a polynomial p by repeatedly applying orthogonal similarity transformations to its companion matrix A until we obtain a matrix in reduced upper Hessenberg form. The first step is to find a matrix Q to use in the orthogonal similarity transformation. We can always express any real matrix A as the product of an orthogonal matrix Q and an upper triangular matrix R; this is called the QR decomposition of A. Once we have Ak = QkRk, we form Ak+1 = QkTAkQk = RkQk. There are some choices for how to perform the decomposition.

2.4.1. Triangular Orthogonalization. The most famous technique, known as the Gram-Schmidt algorithm, is to iteratively form the columns of Q from the columns of A: Q = AR-1 in such a way that R is upper triangular. Since Q is the result of many operations, this method has poor numerical stability when using finite-precision arithmetic on a computer; Q will not be exactly orthogonal, so QT will not be Q-1.

2.4.2. Orthogonal Triangularization. A better method for use in computers is to form the upper triangular matrix R as R = QnTQ1TA, where each Qj is an orthogonal matrix that sets all entries below the main diagonal in the jth column of A to zero. Now A = QR with Q = Q1Qn. There are two possible forms for the Qj: Householder reflections and Givens rotations. A Householder reflection is a reflection of a vector about a hyperplane through the origin. One can be represented by the symmetric orthogonal matrix H = I - 2vvT, where v is the normal vector of the hyperplane. To bring A to upper triangular form using Householder reflections, we zero out the subdiagonal elements of each column. For the first column, we want to produce a vector in the span of e1, the canonical unit basis vector with a 1 in the first entry and zeros elsewhere. Let a1 be the first column of A, and form u = a1 -e1. Normalizing to form v = produces the normal to an appropriate hyperplane. Note that we only need to set to zero the entries we know are non-zero, and we know all entries of A below the first subdiagonal are already zero. Convergence


The basic QR iteration computes Ak+1 = QkTAkQk; here A0 is the companion matrix of the polynomial we want to solve, and Qk is obtained from the QR decomposition of Ak. This step can be computed with O operations, where n is the degree of the polynomial. The question is if Ak will converge to a canonical form, and if so, how many iterations we expect it to take. For reduced upper Hessenberg form, we need subdiagonal elements of Ak to become zero; no two consecutive diagonal elements can be non-zero. Once a sudiagonal element has been brought close enough to zero (a metric that will be made precise in section 4), we say the 1 × 1 or 2 × 2 matrix to the right of this element has become detached from the rest of Ak and we can compute its eigenvalues. We no longer need to update this part of the matrix with QR iterations, so we effectively reduce the dimension of the matrix; this is called deflation. We can measure the extent to which Ak fails to be in reduced upper Hessenberg form by the largest magnitude of its subdiagonal elements. 3.3. Double Implicit Shift. The implicit Q theorem states that if QTAQ = H and V TAV = G are both upper Hessenberg matrices for a general matrix A and orthogonal matrices Q and V with the same first column, then H and G are equal up to a change of sign. Thus we don’t need to compute Mk and its QR factorization; we can compute a matrix Zk′ with the same first column as Zk above such that Ak+1 = Zk′TAkZk′ is upper Hessenberg. The prescription to compute Zk′ is as follows:

  • compute the first column of Mk;

  • compute a Householder matrix P0 which zeros out all but the first entry of this column;

  • compute Householder matrices P1,…,Pn-2 such that Zk′ = P0P1Pn-2 satisfies the required properties.

The first and second steps requires a constant number of operations. To perform the last step, note that since P0 is a 3 × 3 Householder transformation, the first three columns P0TAkP0 will have as many non-zero entries as the third column of Ak. This requires P1 to be a 3 × 3 Householder transformation as well to zero out the 2 subdiagonal elements below the first subdiagonal of P0TAkP0, giving the second, third, and fourth columns of P1TP0TAkP0P1 the same number of non-zero entries as the fourth column of P0TAkP0, and so on. Thus each of P1,…,Pn-3 will be a 3 × 3 Householder transformation, while Pn-2 only needs to be 2 × 2. Zeroing out the one or two entries below the subdiagonal in each column is sometimes refered to as bulge chasing in the literature. Applicability of the theorem then follows from the fact that P0 and Zk have the same first column and P1,…,Pn-2 don’t modify the first column. Multiplications with each of the matrices forming Zk′ can be computed in constant time, so the total iteration can be completed with O operations.

4.1. Coalescing Higher-Order Roots. Our data model stores roots along with their orders. As the QR algorithm deflates, roots are added to the solution set with order at most 2. It is therefore necessary to coalesce individual roots corresponding to the same higher-order root. In practice, as the order of a root increases, the approximated individual roots lie on a circle of increasing radius centered at the true root. We therefore require a post-processing algorithm that will coalesce approximate root corresponding to the same high-order true root while leaving truly distinct roots alone.

Our algorithm keeps a running state of the current root cluster. As roots are deflated, we prospectively combine them with the cluster using a weighted averge. When adding a real root, the result is taken to be real (weighting an existing complex root with twice the order and zero imaginary part, as if we had combined it with its conjugate). We check if the new cluster is a better solution than the previous cluster by repeatedly dividing the polynomial by each root; this generates two sequences of remainders which are the values of the polynomial and its derivatives up to the cluster order evaluated at either cluster center. The new cluster is declared a stronger approximation of the true root if each of the corresponding remainders is not much larger than those corresponding to the old cluster; we have found 3 orders of magnitude to be a sufficient tolerance. Since the new cluster has a higher order than the old cluster, there will be at least one remainder that we do not compare. In the case that the new cluster is near a true root but its order is higher than that of the true root, the extra remainders will be large. For this reason we have a second check that each remainder is not much larger than the one preceding it; we use a tolerance of 8000.

These two checks mostly ensure robust behavior. The serial nature of computing successive remainders of polynomials makes it difficult to take advantage of parallel, superscalar hardware, but this has not been observed to have a significant impact on the total runtime of the solver. It should be noted that this method is not fully immune to erroneous behavior. It is possible the algorithm successively deflates two approximate roots which each correspond to a true isolated root and whose weighted average is another true root with the same or greater order as the prospective cluster. In this case the post-processing algorithm would classify the new cluster as a better approximation than the previous one, mistakenly omitting the isolated roots and adding too much contribution to the higher-order root at their midpoint. While possible, we have not observed this to happen in practice.
