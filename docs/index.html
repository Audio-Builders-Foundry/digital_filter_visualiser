Digital Filter Visualizer

an intuitive way to create, visualize and evaluate your digital filter

ABSTRACT

Digital filters are a foundational concept in digital audioIs this working? , but designing them presents certain challenges: from finding the right coefficients to checking for stability. To facilitate the study of this topic, it would be highly beneficial to have a tool with an interactive visual interface. Such a tool should allow users to determine which roots of the polynomials defining a filter correspond to given coefficients - and vice versa - as well as to adjust these roots on the complex plane in real time, instantly hearing how the audio processing result changes and observing the corresponding shifts in the filter's frequency and phase response. This would help foster an intuitive grasp of the processes underlying the mathematical formulas.

We present Digital Filter Visualizer: a tool that allows you to see the mathematical structure of linear time-invariant filters, calculate filter coefficients from your poles and zeros and vice versa, automatically ensure the stability, and instantly hear the result.

Digital Filter Visualizer features 5 autosynchronized representations of a filter, including interactive visual interfaces, to provide utility in both learning and creating.

In this poster, we concentrate on the math behind the most challenging part of its implementation - namely, converting the filter's coefficients to roots, that is provided using the Francis implicit double shift QR algorithm. Other problems, like building the filter's processing chain and plotting frequency response, will be briefly described as well.

The open-source implementation of Digital Filter Visualizer as a standalone app as well as a VST3 plug-in available at: https://www.github.com/Audio-Builders-Foundry/digital_filter_visualiser.

Technical Preliminaries

An IIR filter is defined by its transfer function:


H(z)=gB(z)A(z),H(z) = g\frac{B(z)}{A(z)},
B(z)=(1−p1z−1)(1−p2z−1)…(1−pMz−1),B(z) = \left( 1 - p_{1}z^{- 1} \right)\left( 1 - p_{2}z^{- 1} \right)\ldots\left( 1 - p_{M}z^{- 1} \right),
A(z)=(1−q1z−1)(1−q2z−1)…(1−qNz−1),A(z) = \left( 1 - q_{1}z^{- 1} \right)\left( 1 - q_{2}z^{- 1} \right)\ldots\left( 1 - q_{N}z^{- 1} \right),

where z is the complex variable of the z-transform, g is the real gain factor, p1…pM are the zeros, and q1…qN are the poles.

Computing the polynomial coefficients from known roots is a simple task: we only need to multiply out the brackets in the numerator and the denominator. The numerical error can be reduced by choosing the order in which the brackets are multiplied out: from the roots with a smaller magnitude to the roots with a larger magnitude.

The inverse problem, however, is much more complex.

Computing Roots from Coefficients

1. OVERVIEW

There are no closed-form solutions for polynomials of degree 5 or greater; iterative methods are required. Here we will discuss the different methods for solving polynimials (Fig.1). Algorithms largely fall into one of two broad categories: Newton’s method-based solvers and eigenvalue solvers.

Fig.1 Overview of the methods discussed in this poster

Newton’s method is a technique for finding roots of any function, not just polynomials. While it is relatively simple and can be applied to polynomials as well, Newton’s method has problems with convergence, especially when dealing with complex roots and roots with multiplicity. There are specializations of Newton’s method for polynomials that try to address these problems, such as the Aberth-Ehrlich method, but we found these insufficient.

Eigenvalue solvers use a method for computing the eigenvalues of a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. Compared to Newton solvers, they require more sophisticated mathematical theory to understand, and are seemingly more computationaly expensive; indeed the operations on polynomials in Newton’s method can be implemented in linear time, whereas most operations on matrices require quadratic or cubic time. Yet the eigenvalue formalism offers advantages in analyzing convergence and designing algorithms with improved performance on roots with multiplicity.

We use an Eigenvalue solver using a variant of the QR algorithm called the Francis double implicit shift. Simple QR algorithm implementations have issues with convergence for complex roots and roots with multiplicity, much like Newton solvers, which can be addressed using a technique called shifting. Single shifting is insufficient due to the possibility of complex roots, which necessitates double shifting; explicit double shifting is inefficient, hence the need for implicit double shifts. Orthogonal matrices are constructed as a product of Householder reflections.

The QR solver with double implicit shift finds one or two roots at a time. Our application stores roots along with their orders. When the polynomial has roots with order more than 1, the solver will produce several roots whose weighted average is a close approximation of the true root. Individual roots corresponding to the same high order root may not appear consecutively in the list of solutions, and each root will be farther from the true root position the higher its order. This necessitates a post-processing pass to group individual roots corresponding to the same true root into a single object.

Section 2 describes how the QR eigenvalue solver works in general. Section 3 details methods for improving the convergence rate of the general QR solver. Section 4 contains details about our specific implementation and the post processing technique for clustering higher-order roots.

2. STRATEGY

We will construct a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. Then we will iteratively apply operations to this matrix in such a way that we don’t change its eigenvalues, until we have produced a matrix whose eigenvalues we can determine directly from its coefficients.

2.1. Companion Matrix

To start, we need to construct a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial we want to solve. This can be done as follows: given a polynomial p(x)=anxn+…+a0{p(x) = a_nx^n + \dots + a_0}, the matrix

A=[00⋯0−a010⋯0−a101⋯0−a2⋮⋮⋱⋮⋮00⋯1−an−1]\begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_0 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_1 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -a_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -a_{n-1} \\ \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
has eigenvalues λ1,…,λn\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n such that p(x)=(x−λ1)⋯(x−λn){p(x) = (x - \lambda_1)\cdots(x - \lambda_n)}. The matrix AA is called the companion matrix of the polynomial pp. Note that the companion matrix has ones along the diagonal below the main diagonal and zeros in all places below this subdiagonal; we call any matrix with this property an upper Hessenberg matrix, or more specifically, an unreduced upper Hessenberg matrix. Finding the eigenvalues of upper Hessenberg matrices is a well-studied problem, and exploiting this structure will play a key role in the theory.

2.2. Canonical Forms

We list some matrices whose eigenvalues can be determined directly from its coefficients. The simplest of these are diagonal matrices:

D=[d10⋯00d2⋯0⋮⋮⋱⋮00⋯dn]\begin{equation*} D = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & d_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_n \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
whose eigenvalues are simply their diagonal entries. The same is true for upper triangular matrices:

T=[t11t12⋯t1n0t22⋯t2n⋮⋮⋱⋮00⋯tnn].\begin{equation*} T = \begin{bmatrix} t_{11} & t_{12} & \cdots & t_{1n} \\ 0 & t_{22} & \cdots & t_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & t_{nn} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
These matrices can be targeted when finding the eigenvalues of a matrix with complex entries (diagonal when the matrix is hermitian, upper triangular otherwise). Since we are working with polynomials with real coefficients, and therefore matrices with real entries, we will need to target a slightly less intuitive form, which we call block upper triangular:

T=[T11T12⋯T1n0T22⋯T2n⋮⋮⋱⋮00⋯Tnn]\begin{equation*} T = \begin{bmatrix} T_{11} & T_{12} & \cdots & T_{1n} \\ 0 & T_{22} & \cdots & T_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & T_{nn} \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
where each TijT_{ij} is a square matrix, all matrices in the same row have the same number of rows, all matrices in the same column have the same number of columns, and the zeros denote zero matrices. The set of eigenvalues of a matrix of this form is the union of the sets of eigenvalues of each matrix along the main diagonal. A block upper triangular matrix made up of either 1×11 \times 1 or 2×22 \times 2 matrices is known as a reduced upper Hessenberg matrix. Our goal is to bring the unreduced upper Hessenberg companion matrix to reduced upper Hessenberg form by bringing some subdiagonal entries to zero.

2.3. Similar Matrices

Two square matrices AA and BB with the same dimension have the same eigenvalues whenever there is a matrix XX such that B=X−1AXB = X^{-1} A X; we say AA and BB are similar matrices in this case. Given a matrix AA, forming B=X−1AXB = X^{-1} A X for some matrix XX is called conjugation of A by X. We will refer to this operation as applying a similarity transformation to AA. A matrix QQ is called orthogonal if QT=Q−1Q^T = Q^{-1}; these are a good choice for use in similarity transformations since it avoids having to form the inverse of a matrix (an expensive operation in general). We will call a similarity transformation using an orthogonal matrix an orthogonal similarity transformation.

2.4. QR Decomposition

So we want to find the roots of a polynomial pp by repeatedly applying orthogonal similarity transformations to its companion matrix AA until we obtain a matrix in reduced upper Hessenberg form. The first step is to find a matrix QQ to use in the orthogonal similarity transformation. We can always express any real matrix AA as the product of an orthogonal matrix QQ and an upper triangular matrix RR; this is called the QR decomposition of AA. Once we have Ak=QkRkA_k = Q_k R_k, we form Ak+1=QkTAkQk=RkQkA_{k+1} = Q_k^T A_k Q_k = R_k Q_k. There are some choices for how to perform the decomposition (Fig 2).

Fig.2 QR Decomposition

2.4.1. Triangular Orthogonalization

The most famous technique, known as the Gram-Schmidt algorithm, is to iteratively form the columns of QQ from the columns of AA: Q=AR−1Q = A R^{-1} in such a way that RR is upper triangular. Since QQ is the result of many operations, this method has poor numerical stability when using finite-precision arithmetic on a computer; QQ will not be exactly orthogonal, so QTQ^T will not be Q−1Q^{-1}.

2.4.2. Orthogonal Triangularization

A better method for use in computers is to form the upper triangular matrix RR as R=QnT⋯Q1TAR = Q_n^T \cdots Q_1^T A, where each QjQ_j is an orthogonal matrix that sets all entries below the main diagonal in the jthj^{\mathrm{th}} column of AA to zero. Now A=QRA = Q R with Q=Q1⋯QnQ = Q_1 \cdots Q_n.

There are two possible forms for the QjQ_j: Householder reflections and Givens rotations.

1. Householder Reflection

A Householder reflection is a reflection of a vector about a hyperplane through the origin. One can be represented by the symmetric orthogonal matrix H=I−2vvTH = I - 2vv^T, where vv is the normal vector of the hyperplane.

To bring AA to upper triangular form using Householder reflections, we zero out the subdiagonal elements of each column. For the first column, we want to produce a vector in the span of e1\mathrm{e}_1, the canonical unit basis vector with a 1 in the first entry and zeros elsewhere. Let a1a_1 be the first column of AA, and form u=a1−∥a1∥e1u = a_1 - \left\lVert a_1 \right\rVert e_1. Normalizing to form v=u∥u∥v = \frac{u}{\left\lVert u \right\rVert} produces the normal to an appropriate hyperplane. Note that we only need to set to zero the entries we know are non-zero, and we know all entries of AA below the first subdiagonal are already zero. Thus we may treat aja_j, e1e_1, uu, and vv as 2-component vectors for each of the first n−1n-1 columns. This means that multiplication with QjQ_j only requires a constant number of operations per column, and so the update requires O(n2)O\left( n^2 \right) operations.

2. Givens Rotation

A Givens rotation is a rotation in the plane spanned by two basis vectors. We can bring a single non-zero subdiagonal element of a column of AA to zero by

[c−ssc][aj,jaj+1,j]=[r0]\begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_{j,j} \\ a_{j+1,j} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
where aj,ja_{j,j} and aj+1,ja_{j+1,j} are the diagonal and subdiagonal elements of the jthj^{\mathrm{th}} column of AA, r=aj,j+aj+1,jr = \sqrt{a_{j,j} + a_{j+1,j}}, c=aj,jrc = \frac{a_{j,j}}{r}, and s=−aj+1,jrs = -\frac{a_{j+1,j}}{r}. In 3.3, we will require operations that bring 2 subdiagonal entries to zero instead of 1. For this reason, it is more convenient to work with Householder reflections. We never implemented a version of the QR algorithm using Givens rotations, and so cannot comment on whether any of their potential advantages can be realized in practice.

3. CONVERGENCE

The basic QR iteration computes Ak+1=QkTAkQkA_{k+1} = Q_k^T A_k Q_k; here A0A_0 is the companion matrix of the polynomial we want to solve, and QkQ_k is obtained from the QR decomposition of AkA_k. This step can be computed with O(n2)O\left( n^2 \right) operations, where nn is the degree of the polynomial. The question is if AkA_k will converge to a canonical form, and if so, how many iterations we expect it to take. For reduced upper Hessenberg form, we need subdiagonal elements of AkA_k to become zero; no two consecutive diagonal elements can be non-zero. Once a sudiagonal element has been brought close enough to zero (a metric that will be made precise in 4), we say the 1×11 \times 1 or 2×22 \times 2 matrix to the right of this element has become detached from the rest of AkA_k and we can compute its eigenvalues. We no longer need to update this part of the matrix with QR iterations, so we effectively reduce the dimension of the matrix; this is called deflation. We can measure the extent to which AkA_k fails to be in reduced upper Hessenberg form by the largest magnitude of its subdiagonal elements. Analysis shows this magnitude of this element is O(|λ2|k|λ1|k)O\left( \frac{\left\vert \lambda_2 \right\vert^k}{\left\vert \lambda_1 \right\vert^k} \right), where λ1,…,λn\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n are the eigenvalues of AkA_k ordered such that |λ1|≥|λ2|≥…≥|λn|\left\vert \lambda_1 \right\vert \geq \left\vert \lambda_2 \right\vert \geq \dots \geq \left\vert \lambda_n \right\vert. Thus if |λ1|=|λ2|\left\vert \lambda_1 \right\vert = \left\vert \lambda_2 \right\vert, which will occur in the presence of a multiple root or a conjugate pair of roots, the iterations will not converge; if |λ1|−|λ2|\left\vert \lambda_1 \right\vert - \left\vert \lambda_2 \right\vert is very small, convergence will be very slow. We are therefore motivated to modify the strategy to speed up convergence.

Fig.3 Methods for reducing amount of iterations

3.1. Shifted QR

A popular modification is to perform shifting: form Ak̃=Ak−μI{\widetilde{A_k} = A_k - \mu I} for some number μ\mu, compute Ak+1̃=QkTAk̃Qk\widetilde{A_{k+1}} = Q_k^T \widetilde{A_k} Q_k obtaining QkQ_k from the QR factorization of Ak̃\widetilde{A_k}, then obtain Ak+1=Ak+1̃+μIA_{k+1} = \widetilde{A_{k+1}} + \mu I. If λ1,…,λn\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n are the eigenvalues of AkA_k, then λ1−μ,…λn−μ\lambda_1 - \mu, \dots \lambda_n - \mu are the eigenvalues of Ak̃\widetilde{A_k}; when the eigenvalues are ordered |λ1|≥…≥|λn|\left\vert \lambda_1 \right\vert \geq \dots \geq \left\vert \lambda_n \right\vert, we see the largest magnitude of a subdiagonal element of AkA_k is now O(|λ2−μ|k|λ1−μ|k)O\left( \frac{\left\vert \lambda_2 - \mu \right\vert^k}{\left\vert \lambda_1 - \mu \right\vert^k} \right). When μ\mu is approximately λ2\lambda_2, convergence will be very fast. Often the bottom-right entry of AkA_k is chosen as an approximation.

3.2. Double Shifted QR

When λ2\lambda_2 is complex, no entry of AkA_k will serve as a good approximation since the entries are all real. We also don’t want to shift by a complex number, for then the entries of the matrix will be complex. Instead we perform two shifts:

Ak̃1=Ak−a1I=Qk1Rk1,Ak1=Rk1Qk1+a1I;Ak̃2=Ak1−a2I=Qk2Rk2,Ak2=Rk2Qk2+a2I.\begin{align*} \widetilde{A_k}_1 &= A_k - a_1I \\ &= {Q_k}_1 {R_k}_1, \\ {A_k}_1 &= {R_k}_1 {Q_k}_1 + a_1I; \\ \widetilde{A_k}_2 &= {A_k}_1 - a_2I \\ &= {Q_k}_2 {R_k}_2, \\ {A_k}_2 &= {R_k}_2 {Q_k}_2 + a_2I. \end{align*}
We see

(Qk1Qk2)(Rk2Rk1)=Qk1(Ak1−a2I)Rk1=Qk1(Rk1Qk1+a1I−a2I)Rk1=(Ak−a1I)(Ak−a1I)+a1(Ak−a1I)−a2(Ak−a1I)=(Ak−a1I)(Ak−a1I+a1I−a2I)=(Ak−a1I)(Ak−a2I)=Ak2−sAk+tI=:Mk,\begin{align*} \left( {Q_k}_1 {Q_k}_2 \right)\left( {R_k}_2 {R_k}_1 \right) &= {Q_k}_1\left( {A_k}_1 - a_2I \right){R_k}_1 \\ &= {Q_k}_1\left( {R_k}_1{Q_k}_1 + a_1I - a_2I \right){R_k}_1 \\ &= \left( A_k - a_1I \right)\left( A_k - a_1I \right) + a_1\left( A_k - a_1I \right) - a_2\left( A_k - a_1I \right) \\ &= \left( A_k - a_1I \right)\left( A_k - a_1I + a_1I - a_2I \right) \\ &= \left( A_k - a_1I \right)\left( A_k - a_2I \right) \\ &= A_k^2 - sA_k + tI \\ &=: M_k, \end{align*}
where s=a1+a2s = a_1 + a_2 and t=a1a2t = a_1a_2 for shifts a1,a2a_1, a_2. a1a_1 and a2a_2 are usually taken to be the eigenvalues of the bottom-right 2×22 \times 2 diagonal block HH of AkA_k; we can then compute ss and tt directly from the coefficients of HH as s=tr(H)s = \mathrm{tr}\left( H \right), and t=det(H)t = \mathrm{det}\left( H \right). Since ss and tt are real, it is possible to choose Qk1{Q_k}_1 and Qk2{Q_k}_2 such that Zk=Qk1Qk2Z_k = {Q_k}_1 {Q_k}_2 is a real orthogonal matrix. Thus we may execute the double shifted procedure

Mk=Ak2−sAk+tI,=ZkRk with Zk real,Ak+1=ZkTAkZk.\begin{align*} M_k &= A_k^2 - sA_k + tI, \\ &= Z_k R_k \text{ with } Z_k \text{ real}, \\ A_{k+1} &= Z_k^T A_k Z_k. \end{align*}
Unfortunately, calculating Ak2A_k^2 requires O(n3)O\left( n^3 \right) operations. We can however exploit a result of upper Hessenberg matrices to avoid the explicit matrix multiplication.

3.3. Double Implicit Shift

The implicit Q theorem states that if QTAQ=HQ^T A Q = H and VTAV=GV^T A V = G are both upper Hessenberg matrices for a general matrix AA and orthogonal matrices QQ and VV with the same first column, then HH and GG are equal up to a change of sign. Thus we don’t need to compute MkM_k and its QR factorization; we can compute a matrix Zk′Z_k' with the same first column as ZkZ_k above such that Ak+1=Zk′TAkZk′A_{k+1} = Z_k'^T A_k Z_k' is upper Hessenberg.

The prescription to compute Zk′Z_k' is as follows:

  • compute the first column of MkM_k;

  • compute a Householder matrix P0P_0 which zeros out all but the first entry of this column;

  • compute Householder matrices P1,…,Pn−2P_1, \dots, P_{n-2} such that Zk′=P0P1⋯Pn−2Z_k' = P_0 P_1 \cdots P_{n-2} satisfies the required properties.

The first and second steps requires a constant number of operations. To perform the last step, note that since P0P_0 is a 3×33 \times 3 Householder transformation, the first three columns P0TAkP0P_0^T A_k P_0 will have as many non-zero entries as the third column of AkA_k. This requires P1P_1 to be a 3×33 \times 3 Householder transformation as well to zero out the 2 subdiagonal elements below the first subdiagonal of P0TAkP0P_0^T A_k P_0, giving the second, third, and fourth columns of P1TP0TAkP0P1P_1^T P_0^T A_k P_0 P_1 the same number of non-zero entries as the fourth column of P0TAkP0P_0^T A_k P_0, and so on. Thus each of P1,…,Pn−3P_1, \dots, P_{n-3} will be a 3×33 \times 3 Householder transformation, while Pn−2P_{n-2} only needs to be 2×22 \times 2. Zeroing out the one or two entries below the subdiagonal in each column is sometimes refered to as bulge chasing in the literature. Applicability of the theorem then follows from the fact that P0P_0 and ZkZ_k have the same first column and P1,…,Pn−2P_1, \dots, P_{n-2} don’t modify the first column. Multiplications with each of the matrices forming Zk′Z_k' can be computed in constant time, so the total iteration can be completed with O(n2)O\left( n^2 \right) operations.

4. IMPLEMENTATION

We use the double implicit shift QR algorithm. All matrices are stored in contiguous arrays of doubles. The threshold for decoupling is

|ai,i−1|≤ϵ(|ai,i|+|ai−1,i−1|),\begin{equation*} \left\vert a_{i,i-1} \right\vert \leq \epsilon \left( \left\vert a_{i,i} \right\vert + \left\vert a_{i-1,i-1} \right\vert \right), \end{equation*}
where the ai,ja_{i,j} are entries in the matrix AkA_k and ϵ\epsilon = std::numeric_limits<double>::epsilon(). We have a maximum of 100 iterations before we force a deflate. ϵ\epsilon is chosen so this practically never happens.

4.1. Coalescing Higher-Order Roots

Our data model stores roots along with their orders. As the QR algorithm deflates, roots are added to the solution set with order at most 2. It is therefore necessary to coalesce individual roots corresponding to the same higher-order root. In practice, as the order of a root increases, the approximated individual roots lie on a circle of increasing radius centered at the true root. We therefore require a post-processing algorithm that will coalesce approximate root corresponding to the same high-order true root while leaving truly distinct roots alone.

Our algorithm keeps a running state of the current root cluster. As roots are deflated, we prospectively combine them with the cluster using a weighted averge. Whben adding a real root, the result is taken to be real (weighting an existing complex root with twice the order and zero imaginary part, as if we had combined it with its conjugate). We check if the new cluster is a better solution than the previous cluster by repeatedly dividing the polynomial by each root; this generates two sequences of remainders which are the values of the polynomial and its derivatives up to the cluster order evaluated at either cluster center. The new cluster is declared a stronger approximation of the true root if each of the corresponding remainders is not much larger than those corresponding to the old cluster; we have found 3 orders of magnitude to be a sufficient tolerance. Since the new cluster has a higher order than the old cluster, there will be at least one remainder that we do not compare. In the case that the new cluster is near a true root but its order is higher than that of the true root, the extra remainders will be large. For this reason we have a second check that each remainder is not much larger than the one preceding it; we use a tolerance of 8000.

These two checks mostly ensure robust behavior. The serial nature of computing successive remainders of polynomials makes it difficult to take advantage of parallel, superscalar hardware, but this has not been observed to have a significant impact on the total runtime of the solver. It should be noted that this method is not fully immune to erroneous behavior. It is possible the algorithm successively deflates two approximate roots which each correspond to a true isolated root and whose weighted average is another true root with the same or greater order as the prospective cluster. In this case the post-processing algorithm would classify the new cluster as a better approximation than the previous one, mistakenly omitting the isolated roots and adding too much contribution to the higher-order root at their midpoint. While possible, we have not observed this to happen in practice.



Building a filter processing chain

High-order polynomials are acceptable for displaying the solution. However, if the filter is implemented as a single block, that is, as the ratio of these polynomials, the solution becomes numerically unstable. The coefficients are computed with finite precision, and the effect of rounding errors grows with the polynomial order. Besides a simple mismatch between the theoretical and the actual filter parameters, this increases the risk of instability: in theory the filter is stable (its poles are inside the unit circle), but rounding errors in the coefficients can make the actual filter unstable.

To improve accuracy and reduce the risk of instability, the filter is implemented as a cascade of biquads, that is, second-order IIR filters connected in series. Second order is the lowest order that can represent complex-conjugate roots. In this form, the pole sensitivity (the ratio of the pole shift caused by an error in a coefficient to the error itself) stays minimal.

The effect of coefficient errors on the filter parameters can be reduced further by choosing which zeros and poles go into each biquad.



First, we need to find which poles can add the largest error to the processed signal. The average absolute error of a floating-point number is proportional to the value of this number. Therefore, the potential error of a filter can be estimated by its maximum gain, that is, its gain at the peak frequency. If the input signal is unknown, this gain measures the risk in the worst case. Here and below, the gain of a filter at a given frequency means the magnitude of the transfer function |H(z)| at the point z of the unit circle that corresponds to this frequency.

Finding the peak frequency

If g = 1 and the filter has only poles, the maximum gain is reached at the frequency where the denominator of the transfer function is minimal:


max|H(z)|=max|B(z)||A(z)|=1min|A(z)|.\max\left| H(z) \right| = \max\frac{\left| B(z) \right|}{\left| A(z) \right|} = \frac{1}{\min\left| A(z) \right|}.

Here the maximum and the minimum are taken over the unit circle, so z is a point of this circle at the angle ω to the real axis: z = (cos(ω), sin(ω)). In turn, ω is the angular frequency, computed as follows:

ω=2πffs\omega = \frac{2\pi f}{f_s}

where f is the frequency in Hz, and fs is the sampling rate (s−1).

If the filter has a single real pole q with polar coordinates (r, 0) or (r, π), then

min|A(z)|=min|A(z)|2.\min\left| A(z) \right| = \sqrt{\min\left| A(z) \right|^{2}}.

Since |z|=1|z| = 1, we have z−1=z¯|z|2=z¯z^{- 1} = \frac{\overline{z}}{|z|^{2}} = \overline{z}.

|A(z)|2=A(z)A(z)¯=1+a12+a22+a1(z+z−1)+a1a2(z+z−1)+a2(z2+z−2)=1+a12+a22+2a1cos(ω)+2a1a2cos(ω)+2a2cos(2ω)=1+a12+a22+2a1(1+a2)cos(ω)+2a2cos(2ω)=1+a12+a22+2a1(1+a2)cos(ω)+2a2(2cos2(ω)−1)=(1−a2)2+a12+2a1(1+a2)cos(ω)+4a2cos2(ω).\begin{array}{r} \left| A(z) \right|^{2} = A(z)\overline{A(z)} \\ = 1 + a_{1}^{2} + a_{2}^{2} + a_{1}\left( z + z^{- 1} \right) + a_{1}a_{2}\left( z + z^{- 1} \right) + a_{2}\left( z^{2} + z^{- 2} \right) \\ = 1 + a_{1}^{2} + a_{2}^{2} + 2a_{1}\,\cos(\omega) + 2a_{1}a_{2}\,\cos(\omega) + 2a_{2}\,\cos(2\omega) \\ = 1 + a_{1}^{2} + a_{2}^{2} + 2a_{1}\left( 1 + a_{2} \right)\,\cos(\omega) + 2a_{2}\,\cos(2\omega) \\ = 1 + a_{1}^{2} + a_{2}^{2} + 2a_{1}\left( 1 + a_{2} \right)\,\cos(\omega) + 2a_{2}\left( 2\,\cos^{2}(\omega) - 1 \right) \\ = \left( 1 - a_{2} \right)^{2} + a_{1}^{2} + 2a_{1}\left( 1 + a_{2} \right)\,\cos(\omega) + 4a_{2}\,\cos^{2}(\omega). \end{array}

Let c = cos(ω). Then |A|2|A|^{2} is a parabola that opens upward:

|A|2=(1−a2)2+a12+2a1(1+a2)c+4a2c2.|A|^{2} = \left( 1 - a_{2} \right)^{2} + a_{1}^{2} + 2a_{1}\left( 1 + a_{2} \right)c + 4a_{2}c^{2}.

Its minimum is reached at

c=−a1(1+a2)4a2=(1+r2)cos(θ)2r.c = - \frac{a_{1}\left( 1 + a_{2} \right)}{4a_{2}} = \frac{\left( 1 + r^{2} \right)\,\cos(\theta)}{2r}.

If −1 ≤ c ≤ 1, the peak frequency is

ω=arccos((1+r2)cos(θ)2r).\omega = \arccos\left( \frac{\left( 1 + r^{2} \right)\,\cos(\theta)}{2r} \right).

Otherwise, the maximum gain of the filter is reached at ω = 0 if c > 1, and at ω = π if c < −1.

The case where the filter has two distinct real poles is not considered here.



If a stage in the chain of biquads adds an error, the following stages can no longer tell the error from the signal, so they cannot improve the signal-to-error ratio. The potential error can be reduced if a level increase is compensated by a level decrease inside the same biquad. To do this, each pole is paired with a zero. Poles with a higher gain at their peak frequency are paired first. From the zeros that do not have a pair yet, we choose the one that gives the strongest attenuation at the peak frequency of the pole. Zeros that amplify the signal at this frequency are not considered as candidates.

If some zeros are left without a pair after this matching, they form an FIR filter placed after the IIR section.


Computing the frequency response

To plot the phase-frequency and magnitude-frequency response, it is necessary to determine the corresponding frequency functions. It is straightforward if the roots are known. Moreover, there is a simple geometric rule for calculating them. The only challenge lies in correctly constructing a continuous phase function, which requires the use of an "unwrapped" phase. A technique for computing it is described below.



The magnitude response and the phase response of the filter at the angular frequency ω are the magnitude and the argument of the transfer function H(z) at the point z of the unit circle at the angle ω to the real axis. The magnitude response in dimensionless units is a(ω) = |H(z)|, and the phase response is arg(H(z)), defined up to a multiple of 2π. With known roots, both are easy to compute as follows.

Since 1 − piz−1 = z−1(z − pi) and 1 − qjz−1 = z−1(z − qj), the transfer function can be written as

H(z)=gzN−M∏i=1M(z−pi)∏j=1N(z−qj).H(z) = gz^{N - M}\frac{\prod_{i = 1}^{M}\left( z - p_{i} \right)}{\prod_{j = 1}^{N}\left( z - q_{j} \right)}.

The magnitude of a product of complex numbers equals the product of their magnitudes, and the argument of a product equals the sum of their arguments, up to a multiple of 2π. On the unit circle, |zN−M| = 1 and arg(zN−M) = (N − M)ω (also up to a multiple of 2π), while arg(g) = 0 for g > 0 and arg(g) = π for g < 0. Therefore, the magnitude response is

a(ω)=|g|∏i=1M|z−pi|∏j=1N|z−qj|,a(\omega) = |g|\frac{\prod_{i = 1}^{M}\left| z - p_{i} \right|}{\prod_{j = 1}^{N}\left| z - q_{j} \right|},

and the phase response, up to a multiple of 2π, is

φ(ω)=(N−M)ω+arg(g)+∑i=1Marg(z−pi)−∑j=1Narg(z−qj).\varphi(\omega) = (N - M)\omega + \arg(g) + \sum_{i = 1}^{M}{\arg\left( z - p_{i} \right)} - \sum_{j = 1}^{N}{\arg\left( z - q_{j} \right)}.

Geometrically, these formulas mean the following. The magnitude response is the product of the lengths of the vectors from the zeros to the point z, divided by the product of the lengths of the vectors from the poles to the point z, and multiplied by |g|. The phase φ(ω) is the sum of the angles between the real axis and the vectors from the zeros, minus the sum of the angles between the real axis and the vectors from the poles, plus the terms (N − M)ω and arg(g).

The problem with the formula for φ(ω) is that each term in the sums is a wrapped phase, that is, a phase limited to the range from −π to π. This distorts the phase response plot: where the curve should break at the edge of the plot area and continue from the opposite edge, it crosses the horizontal axis instead. Therefore, it is better to plot the unwrapped phase φu(ω). This is a continuous function that satisfies the following condition for all ω:

φu(ω)=φ(ω)+2πk(ω),\varphi_{u}(\omega) = \varphi(\omega) + 2\pi k(\omega),

where k(ω) takes integer values for every ω.

Let φi(ω) = arg(z − pi).

The function arg takes values from −π to π and has a discontinuity when its argument is (x, 0), where x < 0. Therefore, if the argument of arg lies in the right half-plane and is continuous over the whole domain, the value of arg is also continuous. To find φu,i(ω), we need to consider the following cases:

1. If the zero pi is inside the unit circle, then

z−pi=z−pizz¯=z(1−piz¯),z - p_{i} = z - p_{i}z\overline{z} = z\left( 1 - p_{i}\overline{z} \right),
arg(z−pi)=arg(z)+arg(1−piz¯)+2πk(ω)arg(z−pi)=ω+arg(1−piz¯)+2πk(ω).\begin{aligned} \arg(z-p_i) &= \arg(z)+\arg(1-p_i\overline{z})+2\pi k(\omega) \\ &= \omega+\arg(1-p_i\overline{z})+2\pi k(\omega). \end{aligned}

Since |piz¯|<1\left| p_{i}\overline{z} \right| < 1, we have 1−Re(piz¯)>01 - Re\left( p_{i}\overline{z} \right) > 0, and the value of arg is continuous. Therefore, the unwrapped phase for pi can be defined as

φu,i(ω)=ω+arg(1−piz¯).\varphi_{u,i}(\omega) = \omega + \arg\left( 1 - p_{i}\overline{z} \right).

2. If the zero pi is outside the unit circle, then

z−pi=−pi(1−zpi),z - p_{i} = - p_{i}\left( 1 - \frac{z}{p_{i}} \right),
arg(z−pi)=arg(−pi)+arg(1−zpi)+2πk(ω).\arg\left( z - p_{i} \right) = \arg\left( - p_{i} \right) + \arg\left( 1 - \frac{z}{p_{i}} \right) + 2\pi k(\omega).

The value arg(−pi) does not depend on ω. Since |zpi|<1\left| \frac{z}{p_{i}} \right| < 1, we have 1−Re(zpi)>01 - Re\left( \frac{z}{p_{i}} \right) > 0. Thus, the sum of the two terms is continuous, and the unwrapped phase can be defined as

φu,i(ω)=arg(−pi)+arg(1−zpi).\varphi_{u,i}(\omega) = \arg\left( - p_{i} \right) + \arg\left( 1 - \frac{z}{p_{i}} \right).

3. If the zero pi is on the unit circle, the function φu,i(ω) has a discontinuity at ω = arg(pi). If we extend the definition of arg so that arg(0) = 0, then either of the two formulas above can be used to compute φu,i(ω) (of course, the same formula for all ω and all i).

Strictly speaking, if zeros lie on the unit circle, φu,i(ω) is not an unwrapped phase, because it is not continuous. However, this does not matter for plotting the phase response.

The unwrapped phases of the poles are computed in the same way. If the unwrapped phase of the pole qj is denoted by φu,M+j(ω), the total unwrapped phase is

φu(ω)=(N−M)ω+arg(g)+∑i=1Mφu,i(ω)−∑j=1Nφu,M+j(ω).\varphi_{u}(\omega) = (N - M)\omega + \arg(g) + \sum_{i = 1}^{M}{\varphi_{u,i}(\omega)} - \sum_{j = 1}^{N}{\varphi_{u,M + j}(\omega)}.
